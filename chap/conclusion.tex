\chapter{Conclusion and Perspectives}

This thesis investigated the use of logic programming as an implementation
framework for elaboration mechanisms in \rocq, with a particular focus on
type-class resolution and static analyses. More broadly, our goal was to
explore how an external logic-programming engine can be used both as an
implementation tool and as a framework for reasoning about components of an
interactive theorem prover.

A first contribution of this work is the implementation of an alternative
type-class solver in \elpi. We showed that type classes and instances can be
compiled into a logic-programming database using a relatively small amount of
code, while relying on the existing \elpi interpreter for resolution. The
resulting solver shows competitive performance with respect to the native
\rocq implementation. Experimental results presented in
\cref{sec:bench} indicate that the overhead introduced by translating terms
between the object language and the meta-language remains limited in practice,
making this approach viable from an efficiency perspective.

The integration of \rocq and \elpi is, however, not without challenges.
Although both systems support higher-order unification and
$\beta\eta$-conversion rules, their internal representations of terms differ
significantly. This mismatch leads to situations where terms that unify in
\rocq fail to unify after translation into \elpi. To address this issue, we
developed a compilation scheme based on links and delayed constraints. This
approach allows us to recover much of the intended behavior of \rocq
unification while preserving the advantages of the \elpi execution model,
including mode-directed unification.

Overall, the \elpi compiler of \rocq classes and instances consists of
approximately 1,500 lines of code. Around 150 lines are devoted to the
compilation of new predicates when \rocq classes are declared. Roughly 700 lines
implement the infrastructure required to compile rules while introducing links
for problematic subterms, as described in \cref{chap:ho-for-free}.

This infrastructure is organized into three main phases. First, the term
undergoes a preprocessing phase, during which problematic subterms are
identified and replaced with dedicated constructors indicating the category to
which each subterm belongs. The resulting representation is then traversed and
compiled into an \elpi rule or query, depending on whether a \rocq instance or
goal is being translated. Finally, a decompilation phase removes auxiliary
compilation artifacts and reconstructs the final representation expected by the
solver.

The solver itself consists of approximately 200 lines of code dedicated to rule
resolution. In particular, these rules are responsible for translating \rocq
goals into \elpi queries and for managing the link constraints generated during
the instantiation of compiled problematic subterms.

Beyond type-class resolution, this thesis also investigated static analyses for
logic programs. We introduced a determinacy analysis for \elpi capable of
handling higher-order predicates, dynamic clauses, and the cut operator. The
analysis provides guarantees that predicates declared deterministic do not
introduce unnecessary choice points during execution. In the context of
type-class resolution, such guarantees contribute to a better understanding and
control of the search performed by the logic-programming engine.

The static checker is implemented directly in the \elpi backend rather than in
\rocqelpi. Consequently, any \elpi program (including the type-class solver
developed in this thesis) can benefit from it simply by declaring the expected
predicates as deterministic. This illustrates one of the main advantages of the
integration between \elpi and \rocq: techniques developed within the
logic-programming framework can be implemented once in \elpi and subsequently
benefit all systems built on top of it.

The implementation of the static checker relies on discrimination trees built
upon trie-based data structures. Together, these indexing structures account for
approximately 400 lines of code. The two main components of the determinacy
checker — the mutual-exclusion checker and the rule checker — consist of
approximately 200 and 1,000 lines of code, respectively.

To increase confidence in the correctness of our determinacy checker, we have
done a formal verification effort using the \rocq proof assistant. The
current mechanization focuses on a first-order fragment of the \elpi
interpreter. To the best of our knowledge, this constitutes a novel
formalization of the operational semantics of a Prolog-like interpreter
featuring the cut operator and determinacy verification.

Although the formalized model does not capture the full higher-order language,
it is sufficiently expressive to establish machine-checked correctness results
for the determinacy analysis. This work therefore represents a first step toward
a comprehensive formal account of \elpi and of the static analyses developed for
it.

The formalization consists of approximately 7,500 lines of \rocq code, divided
among the specification of the tree-based and stack-based semantics of \elpi,
the formalization of first-order unification based on the Martelli–Montanari
algorithm~\cite{montanari82}, and the implementation and verification of the
static checker.

Taken together, the results presented in this thesis suggest that logic
programming provides a powerful framework for studying, prototyping, and
reasoning about elaboration mechanisms. The integration of \rocq and \elpi
demonstrates that mature logic-programming technology can be effectively
leveraged within theorem-proving environments while maintaining the level of
rigor expected of such systems.

Beyond the specific contributions presented here, several directions for future
work remain open. We detail them in the following perspective section.
