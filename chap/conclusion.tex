\chapter{Conclusion and Perspectives}

This thesis investigated the use of logic programming as an implementation
framework for elaboration mechanisms in \rocq, with a particular focus on
type-class resolution and static analyses. More broadly, our goal was to
explore how an external logic-programming engine can be used both as an
implementation tool and as a framework for reasoning about components of an
interactive theorem prover.

A first contribution of this work is the implementation of an alternative
type-class solver in \elpi. We showed that type classes and instances can be
compiled into a logic-programming database using a relatively small amount of
code, while relying on the existing \elpi interpreter for resolution. The
resulting solver shows competitive performance with respect to the native
\rocq implementation. Experimental results presented in
\cref{sec:bench} indicate that the overhead introduced by translating terms
between the object language and the meta-language remains limited in practice,
making this approach viable from an efficiency perspective.

The integration of \rocq and \elpi is, however, not without challenges.
Although both systems support higher-order unification and
$\beta\eta$-conversion rules, their internal representations of terms differ
significantly. This mismatch leads to situations where terms that unify in
\rocq fail to unify after translation into \elpi. To address this issue, we
developed a compilation scheme based on links and delayed constraints. This
approach allows us to recover much of the intended behavior of \rocq
unification while preserving the advantages of the \elpi execution model,
including mode-directed unification.

Overall, the \elpi compiler of \rocq classes and instances consists of
approximately 1,500 lines of code. Around 150 lines are devoted to the
compilation of new predicates when \rocq classes are declared. Roughly 700 lines
implement the infrastructure required to compile rules while introducing links
for problematic subterms, as described in \cref{chap:ho-for-free}.

This infrastructure is organized into three main phases. First, the term
undergoes a preprocessing phase, during which problematic subterms are
identified and replaced with dedicated constructors indicating the category to
which each subterm belongs. The resulting representation is then traversed and
compiled into an \elpi rule or query, depending on whether a \rocq instance or
goal is being translated. Finally, a decompilation phase removes auxiliary
compilation artifacts and reconstructs the final representation expected by the
solver.

The solver itself consists of approximately 200 lines of code dedicated to rule
resolution. In particular, these rules are responsible for translating \rocq
goals into \elpi queries and for managing the link constraints generated during
the instantiation of compiled problematic subterms.

Beyond type-class resolution, this thesis also investigated static analyses for
logic programs. We introduced a determinacy analysis for \elpi capable of
handling higher-order predicates, dynamic clauses, and the cut operator. The
analysis provides guarantees that predicates declared deterministic do not
introduce unnecessary choice points during execution. In the context of
type-class resolution, such guarantees contribute to a better understanding and
control of the search performed by the logic-programming engine.

The static checker is implemented directly in the \elpi backend rather than in
\rocqelpi. Consequently, any \elpi program (including the type-class solver
developed in this thesis) can benefit from it simply by declaring the expected
predicates as deterministic. This illustrates one of the main advantages of the
integration between \elpi and \rocq: techniques developed within the
logic-programming framework can be implemented once in \elpi and subsequently
benefit all systems built on top of it.

The implementation of the static checker relies on discrimination trees built
upon trie-based data structures. Together, these indexing structures account for
approximately 400 lines of code. The two main components of the determinacy
checker — the mutual-exclusion checker and the rule checker — consist of
approximately 200 and 1,000 lines of code, respectively.

To increase confidence in the correctness of our determinacy checker, we have
done a formal verification effort using the \rocq proof assistant. The
current mechanization focuses on a first-order fragment of the \elpi
interpreter. To the best of our knowledge, this constitutes a novel
formalization of the operational semantics of a Prolog-like interpreter
featuring the cut operator and determinacy verification.

Although the formalized model does not capture the full higher-order language,
it is sufficiently expressive to establish machine-checked correctness results
for the determinacy analysis. This work therefore represents a first step toward
a comprehensive formal account of \elpi and of the static analyses developed for
it.

The formalization consists of approximately 7,500 lines of \rocq code, divided
among the specification of the tree-based and stack-based semantics of \elpi,
the formalization of first-order unification based on the Martelli–Montanari
algorithm~\cite{montanari82}, and the implementation and verification of the
static checker.

Taken together, the results presented in this thesis suggest that logic
programming provides a powerful framework for studying, prototyping, and
reasoning about elaboration mechanisms. The integration of \rocq and \elpi
demonstrates that mature logic-programming technology can be effectively
leveraged within theorem-proving environments while maintaining the level of
rigor expected of such systems.

Beyond the specific contributions presented here, several directions for future
work remain open. We detail them in the following perspective section. 

\section{Perspective}

\subsection{Reusing links for other unification problems}

Despite the improvements presented in \cref{chap:ho-for-free},
unification remains one of the main limitations of our \elpi-based type-class
solver. The techniques developed in that chapter considerably reduce unification
failures, in particular it solves the unification issues coming
from terms in the higher-order pattern fragment modulo
$\beta\eta$-equivalence. However, when evaluating the solver on libraries beyond
\tlc and \stdpp, in particular the \iris~\cite{iris-junk} ecosystem, we
encountered a different class of unification problems arising from the
interaction between type classes and canonical structures~\cite{cs}.

Canonical structures have so far been omitted from the presentation because they
are not part of the type-class mechanism itself, although they are frequently
used to implement forms of symbol overloading. According to the \rocq reference
manual, a canonical structure is \textit{an instance of a record or structure
type that can be used to solve unification problems involving a projection
applied to an unknown structure instance and a value}. Consider the following
example.

Through canonical structure resolution, the unification problem \cI{car ?c =
nat}, where \cI{?c} is an object of type \cI{ofe}, can be solved by consulting
the canonical structure database and retrieving an instance of \cI{ofe} whose
carrier field is \cI{nat}. In contrast to type-class search, canonical structure
resolution is performed during unification and is largely driven by indexing on
the projection being unified, making the process significantly more
deterministic.

Canonical structures are extensively used in libraries such as \hb~\cite{hb}.
However, because their resolution is intertwined with unification, understanding
why a particular instance is selected can be difficult in practice. In
particular, users are typically provided with little information about the
sequence of unification steps that led to a successful resolution. By contrast,
type-class search follows a more explicit resolution procedure and offers
detailed tracing facilities that allow users to inspect both successful and
failed resolution attempts. This increased transparency comes at a cost, as
type-class search is generally slower than canonical structure lookup.

The choice between canonical structures and type classes therefore involves a
trade-off between efficiency and debuggability. Difficulties for our \elpi-based
solver arise precisely when both mechanisms are used together.

For example, the \iris library contains type-class instances parameterized by
projections of canonical structures.

In this example, the instance \cI{c} of the class \cI{C} depends on an instance
of \cI{ofe}. The \elpi compiler translates this declaration into the rule
\eI{tc-C {{car lp:X}} {{c lp:X}}}.

The resulting rule can only be applied when the argument of the class explicitly
exposes the projection \cI{car}. Consequently, our solver is unable to solve the
goal \cI{C nat}. The native \rocq type-class solver, however, succeeds on this
goal because the unification problem \cI{car ?c = nat} triggers canonical
structure resolution, which retrieves the suitable \cI{ofe} instance --
\cI{ofe_nat} in this case -- and allows resolution to proceed.

Addressing this limitation would require extending the compiler so that
occurrences of projections originating from canonical structures are detected
and translated into suitable deferred constraints. The link-based infrastructure
developed in this thesis appears applicable to this setting, as it already
supports delegating difficult unification problems to the constraint solver.
However, identifying such situations statically during compilation remains an
open problem.

An alternative approach would be to reformulate developments so as to rely
exclusively on type classes, thereby eliminating this source of unification
failures altogether. However, as observed in~\cite{assia-exp-blow-up} and
discussed in more detail by
Jung,\footnote{\url{https://www.ralfj.de/blog/2019/05/15/typeclasses-exponential-blowup.html}}
replacing canonical structures with type classes may lead to exponential
blow-ups in search \textit{when using unbundled typeclasses to model algebraic
hierarchies}. Consequently, while such a reformulation would simplify the
interaction with our solver, it may also introduce significant performance
regressions. This highlights a broader tension between expressiveness,
predictability, and search efficiency that warrants further investigation.

\subsection{Tabling}

One of the main advantages coming from using \elpi as a plugin, as already has
been shown by the implementation of the static determinacy checker, is that one
can extend \elpi with features coming from the world of logic programming and
reuse them in the developments base on this language. An interesting aspect of
logic programs is that their search strategy can be extended with a caching
system storing part of the search in the exploration tree. This strategy is
called tabling~\cite{OLD-tabling}. This search strategy ahs been widely adopeted
in the \lean proof assistant~\cite{tabling-dm} and marks a big success in the
resolution of type-classes where, in a hugh mathemathical dependency graph,
several paths may lead to the same solution (or failure). Exploring these
paths may lead to unnecessary computation.  

It would be interesting to see how this searh strategy, once implemented in
\elpi, could impact the exponential blow-up in the instance search, pointed out
in the previous section.

\subsection{Formalizing full \elpi in \rocq}

The formalization we have mechanized concerning the \elpi language and the
determinacy checker seems promising. In our implementation we have already
covered the first-order part of the \elpi language. We have provided a formal
specification of the \elpi semantics together with some of the properties that
this semantics brings. An undoubtely interesting perspective of this work would
be to extend this development so that the higher-order features of the language
are covered. Completing, step by step the \elpi semantics, and proving important
aspects of the language, would prevent erroneus implementations in the \elpi
language, providing therefore a strong guarantee about the language itself.
